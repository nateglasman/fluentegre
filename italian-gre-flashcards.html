What makes these Italian GRE flashcards different

Standard GRE prep materials are built for native English speakers. If you're Italian, you're left memorizing abstract English definitions with no connection to your own language. That's a harder path than it needs to be.

fluenteGRE gives every GRE word an Italian translation, a definition written in Italian, and a memory trick that connects English to Italian. You're studying with your native language as an ally, not studying despite it.

Example: how an Italian GRE flashcard works

Magnanimous
Magnanimo
EN Very generous or forgiving, especially toward a rival or less powerful person
IT Molto generoso o clemente, specialmente verso un rivale o una persona meno potente
Memory trick: "Magna" (grande) + "animo" (spirito) — literally "great spirit." Italian speakers already know both roots. A magnanimous person has a great, generous spirit.

Italian and English share deep Latin roots, and GRE vocabulary is heavily Latin-derived. Words like "equanimity" (equanimita), "recalcitrant" (recalcitrante), and "perspicacious" (perspicace) are practically the same in both languages. fluenteGRE makes these connections explicit across all 1,184 words.

Why Italian speakers need bilingual GRE flashcards

Italy has a growing number of students applying to US and UK graduate programs, and the GRE's Verbal section is often the most daunting part. The irony is that Italian speakers have a hidden advantage: Italian and English share an enormous Latin vocabulary, and the GRE tests exactly those Latin-derived words.

"Pusillanimous" comes from pusillus (very small) + animus (spirit) — an Italian speaker can see "pusillanime" immediately. "Obsequious" connects to "ossequioso." "Loquacious" to "loquace." fluenteGRE doesn't just translate words — it activates the Latin knowledge you already carry as an Italian speaker and turns it into GRE points.
